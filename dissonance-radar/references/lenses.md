# Dissonance lenses

Use this reference when a candidate signal is ambiguous. The examples are fictional. A lens suggests a question; it doesn't establish a motive or diagnosis.

## Goals, roles, process, interpersonal dynamics

### Goals

Look for conflicting outcomes or measures of success, such as one speaker optimizing for an earlier launch while another requires a broader scope.

Ask: "If we can deliver only one of these outcomes by Friday, which matters most?"

Limit: Different suggestions can serve the same goal. Don't label them misalignment without an actual tradeoff in the evidence.

### Roles

Look for incompatible approval decisions, unclear responsibility, or a task that two people each assume the other owns.

Ask: "Who has the final decision on this, and whose input do they need?"

Limit: Offering help doesn't necessarily mean someone is taking over. Ask about ownership instead of declaring a power struggle.

### Process

Look for repeated reopened decisions, incomplete handoffs, or a missing path for handling changes.

Ask: "Where should we record this decision so we can use it next time?"

Limit: A decision may exist outside the supplied material. Describe it as missing from the evidence, not as nonexistent.

### Interpersonal dynamics

Look for observable disagreement, interruption, or communication bypasses. Consider goals, roles, or process explanations before assuming personal conflict.

Ask: "How should we handle it when these two approvals point in different directions?"

Limit: A transcript doesn't establish a person's emotional state or the quality of a relationship. Don't assign blame or diagnose anyone.

## Conversation patterns

### Participation change

A previously expected participant is absent, or their required input is missing. Use the invitation, agenda, or explicit source evidence to establish that participation was expected.

Ask: "Do we need this person's input before deciding?"

Don't flag someone for missing a meeting they were never expected to attend. Absence by itself doesn't demonstrate dissatisfaction.

### In-meeting disagreement

Two directions can't both be followed as stated.

Ask: "I'm hearing one request to keep the current process and another to replace it. Which should guide this version?"

First check whether the speakers are discussing different users, dates, or stages. Apparent contradictions sometimes disappear with context.

### Sentiment or language change

Comparable sources show a speaker changing their explicit assessment, such as moving from "this meets our needs" to "this no longer covers the review step."

Ask: "Has the review requirement changed since our last meeting?"

Quote both sources. Don't infer a shift from "fine" alone, punctuation, a short reply, or missing nonverbal cues.

### Topic left unanswered

A concrete question is followed by a different topic without an answer in the reviewed material.

Ask: "We didn't settle the approval step here. What do we need to decide?"

Say the question was unanswered in the source. Don't assume deliberate avoidance or deception.

### New vocabulary

A consequential term appears in a comparison source, such as "audit requirement" replacing an earlier "nice to have."

Ask: "When you say audit requirement, does that change what must be ready for launch?"

Don't claim a term is new unless the comparison sources support it. In a single transcript, ask what an ambiguous term means instead.

### Commitment follow-through

A prior commitment has a date or condition and later evidence indicates a mismatch or unresolved dependency.

Ask: "Is that handoff still expected before the build starts, or has the plan changed?"

Silence isn't proof of noncompletion. Check known changes or cancellations before treating a promise as overdue.

### Over-owning responsibility

The user accepts a problem as theirs while the source shows unresolved responsibilities or missing inputs from others.

Ask inwardly: "Which part can I act on, and which part needs someone else's decision?"

Stay with the observable work. Don't generalize about the user's personality or assume that shared responsibility removes their part.

### Pace mismatch

The requested scope and deadline conflict with the capacity, dependencies, or budget described in the sources.

Ask: "What should move out of this version to keep the Friday date?"

Use stated constraints. Don't invent capacity estimates or conclude that someone is overworking without evidence.

## Keep or cut?

Keep a signal when the evidence supports the question and the answer could change a real decision. Cut duplicate questions, resolved disagreements, and interpretations that depend on guessing.

If the same pattern appears across several authorized projects, it may be useful for the user's private reflection. Similarity alone doesn't establish a common cause. Don't move one client's confidential details into another client's discussion.
